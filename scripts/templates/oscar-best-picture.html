Best Picture,
by the numbers.

Which Oscar nominee has the best ratings? Which year had the strongest lineup? And when was picking a favorite actually difficult? I put IMDb and Rotten Tomatoes next to each other to see what shook out.

By Ryan Harwick · September 15, 2026
356 nominees · 57 ceremonies · Ratings snapshot: September 15, 2026

This first report covers the 1970–2026 Oscar ceremonies, including every Best Picture nominee and marking the winners. Years below mean ceremony years: the 1970 ceremony honored the 1969 film season, and the 2026 ceremony honored 2025. These are later ratings, collected in September 2026, rather than scores from awards night. Method and sources ↓

Question 01

Which movie comes out on top?

I used three views: IMDb on its own, Rotten Tomatoes on its own, and a combined score. For the combined score, I multiplied IMDb by 10 and averaged it with the Tomatometer percentage. Both get half the weight. It's my comparison rule, with both original numbers still visible.

The Godfather comes out on top at 94.5. Its 9.2 on IMDb and 97% on Rotten Tomatoes put it just ahead of Schindler's List at 94.0. IMDb's individual leader is The Shawshank Redemption, at 9.3. Adding the critics' score changes the order.

At the other end, Hello, Dolly! and Extremely Loud & Incredibly Close tie at 57.0. Rotten Tomatoes' lowest score is also a tie: Anne of the Thousand Days and Hello, Dolly!, both at 43%. IMDb's lowest is Emilia Pérez, at 5.3. That disagreement is part of why I wanted both sources.

{{FILM_RANKINGS}}

Three nominees reach 100% on the Tomatometer: Atlantic City, The Dresser, and A Room with a View. Those scores come from 37, 15, and 37 reviews respectively in this snapshot. A perfect percentage from 15 reviews and one built from hundreds of reviews carry different amounts of evidence; I kept the counts in the downloadable data.

What if I only count the winners?

The Godfather also leads the winners. At the other end, Out of Africa has the lowest combined score, at 67.0, and the lowest Tomatometer score among winners, at 62%. IMDb's lowest-rated winners are Chariots of Fire and Shakespeare in Love, tied at 7.1. So even “lowest-rated Best Picture winner” needs a small follow-up: according to whom?

Question 02

Which year had the strongest lineup?

The 1994 ceremony leads on combined average, at 87.60. That is the lineup of Schindler's List, The Fugitive, In the Name of the Father, The Piano, and The Remains of the Day. Its lowest combined score is still 82.5. The next-highest average belongs to 1973, at 86.50.

Separating the sources changes the answer. IMDb favors 1995, averaging 8.30 across a field that includes The Shawshank Redemption, Pulp Fiction, and Forrest Gump. Rotten Tomatoes favors 1994, averaging 94.8%. The combined-average low is 1970, at 74.20. IMDb's lowest average is 1971, at 7.20, while critics' lowest average is 1970, at 71.6%.

I compare years using the average per nominee. A bigger ballot should not get free points just for being bigger. Each film gets one equal share of its year's score.

{{YEAR_RANKINGS}}

Five films averaging 80 and ten films averaging 80 both score 80 here. Adding the scores and dividing by the number of nominees gives me a comparison on the same scale.

The field grew from five nominees at the 2009 ceremony to ten at the 2010 ceremony. Raw totals mostly hand out points for the extra seats. All three yearly quality measures here use averages instead.

Question 03

Which years were the most competitive?

I used “competitive” to mean close together in ratings. My measure is the spread of the combined scores, calculated with population standard deviation. In plain English: how far do the nominees tend to sit from their year's average? Smaller means a tighter bunch.

2017 is the closest field, with a spread of 1.94 points. All nine nominees fall between 82 and 87. Manchester by the Sea leads this rating measure at 87; Moonlight, the Oscar winner, is at 86. There is very little daylight across that lineup.

1970 has the widest spread, at 13.50 points. Its scores run from 57 for Hello, Dolly! to 87.5 for Z. Anne of the Thousand Days sits at 58.5, while Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid and winner Midnight Cowboy sit above 83. The ratings divide this lineup pretty sharply. “Least competitive” here means the ratings were uneven, not that the winner was obvious to voters.

{{COMPETITION}}

The spread calculation divides the squared differences from the average by the number of nominees before taking the square root. Repeating the same set of scores in a field twice the size gives the same spread. I use the whole field here; a gap between just the top two can shrink simply because there are more films in the running.

This handles the arithmetic of different field sizes. It cannot undo changes in which films get nominated: adding more slots may bring in a different mix of scores. And these are audience and critic ratings, not the Academy's vote margins or what people thought on awards night.

What went into this.

I included every Best Picture nominee from the 1970 through 2026 ceremonies: 356 films, with both scores available for all of them. These are the boundaries of this first report; the rankings aren't claims about the entire history of the Oscars.

I used DLu's Academy nomination dataset to identify the films and their IMDb IDs, with the Academy's ceremony records as the official reference. IMDb scores and vote counts come from IMDb's downloadable ratings file. Each Rotten Tomatoes score comes from its movie page, linked in the table. I matched titles and release years to avoid mixing up remakes and other films with the same name. The source record includes the nomination revision and ratings-file checksum.

I also cross-checked all 356 nominees and 57 winners against Wikipedia's winners and nominees list. They match, allowing for title variations such as Il Postino: The Postman. Wikipedia labels the rows by film-release year; I matched by ceremony number to keep the years aligned. The comparison record is here.

IMDb uses a weighted audience average. The Tomatometer is the percentage of positive critic reviews, not the critics' average mark out of 100. I used the Tomatometer, not Rotten Tomatoes' audience score. Putting both on a similar scale doesn't make them the same kind of measurement.

Film combined score = (IMDb × 10 + Tomatometer) ÷ 2.
Year average = sum of film scores ÷ number of nominees.
Spread = square root of [sum of squared differences from the year's combined average ÷ number of nominees]. This is population standard deviation.

Every film counts equally within its year. I didn't give a heavily rated film extra weight, and I didn't weight by box office or number of reviews. Ties stay tied. Calculations use the recorded scores before rounding the displayed results.

The ratings were collected on September 15, 2026. They can change, especially for newer films, and these audiences aren't a random sample of everyone who watched. The combined ranking also depends on my equal-weight choice. A tiny lead is worth treating as a tiny lead.

My takeaway.

The highest-rated film, the strongest average lineup, and the closest field answer different questions. I want the year comparisons to hold up whether there are five nominees or ten. More slots do not automatically mean better movies.

For this snapshot, I'd point to The Godfather for the combined film score, 1994 for the strongest average, 1970 for the weakest average, and 2017 for the tightest field. Turns out the older nominees weren't going to sit quietly at the bottom of the spreadsheet.
